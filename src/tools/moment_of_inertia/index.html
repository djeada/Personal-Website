1 · What is moment of inertia?

start here

Moment of inertia, written I, measures how hard it is to change how fast something spins. It is the spinning version of mass.

Mass resists a push

Push a cart with a force F and it speeds up with acceleration a. A heavier cart speeds up less. It does not matter where the load sits in the cart.

F = m a

Moment of inertia resists a twist

Twist a wheel with a torque τ and its spin speeds up with angular acceleration α. Larger I, slower spin-up. Here it does matter where the mass sits.

τ = I α

Drag the ball, or focus the picture and use the arrow keys.

One ball on a light arm

The arm weighs nothing. You are looking straight down the axis. The gold square has side r, so its area is r². The ball’s size grows with its mass.

I = m × r²0.250 kg·m²

Twisting applies the same 1 N·m torque to your ball and to the faint starting ball (1 kg at 0.50 m).

Why distance counts twice (r × r)

  1. Farther away means faster. In one turn a ball at distance r travels a circle of length 2πr. To speed up the spin at rate α, the ball must speed up at a = r·α. Twice as far needs twice the push: F = m·r·α.
  2. A twist works through a lever. Torque is force × distance from the axis: τ = r·F = r·(m·r·α) = (m r²)·α.

Compare τ = (m r²)·α with F = m·a: m r² plays the role of mass. That is the moment of inertia of one small ball.

3 · Build the integral, one sum inside another

2D surface · double ∫∫

An integral sign ∫ means “add up”. You need one ∫ for each number it takes to say where a tiny piece is. Each ∫ is a sum, and the sums sit inside each other: first add the pieces along a ring, then add the rings into a slice, then stack the slices.

Smaller pieces: the sum becomes the integral

Piece size Pieces Sum Σ r⊥²Δm Off by

With infinitely many, infinitely small pieces, every Σ turns into an ∫ and the sum equals the exact integral. That limit is all an integral is.

Why do textbooks often show fewer ∫ signs? Symmetry. About a disk’s center axis, every piece in a thin ring has the same distance r, so the sum around the ring is easy: a ring of radius r and width dr has mass σ·2πr·dr. That finishes the inner sum in your head. The integral is still double; the shortcut just does one sum for you. Watch the builder: each completed ring is exactly that shortcut.

5 · What if the axis isn’t through the center?

I = ICM + M d²

Doors spin about their hinges, pendulums about their pivot, a bat about the hands. Spinning about an axis a distance d from the center of mass makes two motions happen at once:

① The center goes around

The center of mass travels around the axis on a circle of radius d. For this motion the body acts like one ball of mass M at distance d, which costs M d² (step 1).

② The body turns about its own center

At the same time it rotates about its own center, exactly as on the centered axis. That costs ICM, the value of the integral.

Looking down the axis animated

Copper ⊙: the axis. Dashed circle: the path of the center. The arrow on the body shows that it also turns about its own center.

The curve is lowest at d = 0 and rises as a parabola. The gold bar is the extra M d².

② Spin about own center ICM
① Center going around M d²
Total I

The center is the easiest axis

M d² is never negative, so of all parallel axes the one through the CM has the smallest I.

Left or right doesn’t matter

d and −d give the same I, because d is squared.

Example: a rod about its end

ML²/12 + M(L/2)² = ML²/3: four times harder to spin than about its middle.

Why the two parts simply add, with nothing in between

Measure each piece from the CM: its position is r′, and the axis sits at d. Its squared distance to the axis is |r′ − d|² = r′² − 2 r′·d + d². Adding over all pieces: Σ m r′² = ICM, Σ m d² = M d², and the middle term is −2 d·Σ m r′ = 0, because Σ m r′ = 0 is exactly what “center of mass” means. Nothing else is left.

What distance is actually squared?

The axis points along n = (sin β, 0, cos β) and is shifted by d(cos β, 0, −sin β). Distance is measured perpendicular to this line, not to a single point on it.

Learn with this tool

Learning objectives

  • Explain moment of inertia as the spinning version of mass, and why distance enters squared.
  • Tell how many integrals a body needs, and read a double or triple integral as sums inside sums.
  • Use I = ICM + M d² for an axis that does not pass through the center.

Prerequisites

  • Newton’s second law F = m a.
  • Area and volume of simple shapes.
  • Helpful, not required: what an integral means as an area under a curve.

Model

Uniform rigid bodies spin about a fixed axis. Every tiny piece contributes its mass times its squared perpendicular distance from the axis, and the moment of inertia is the sum: I = ∫ r⊥² dm. Under a constant torque τ from rest, α = τ/I, ω = αt and θ = ½αt².

Exercise 1 — twice as far

  1. In step 1, the ball sits 0.50 m from the axis. If you move it to 1.00 m, how many times larger is I?
  2. Drag the ball to r = 1.00 m.
  3. Read I and press Twist both.
  4. Why does the ball at 1.00 m turn only a quarter as far in 2 s?
Show answer

4×. I = m r², so doubling r multiplies I by 2² = 4, and α = τ/I is four times smaller.

Exercise 2 — disk or hoop?

  1. Same 2 kg, same 1 m radius. Which spins up faster: a solid disk or a thin hoop?
  2. Choose the experiment “Disk vs. hoop” and apply the twist.
  3. Read both values of I and α.
  4. Use “Where does the inertia come from?” to explain the difference.
Show answer

The disk: I = MR²/2 = 1 kg·m² versus MR² = 2 kg·m² for the hoop. All of the hoop’s mass is at the rim, the farthest place.

Exercise 3 — a double integral by hand

  1. Choose “Build a plate” (4 × 4 pieces). How many strips will the outer sum add, and how many pieces are in each strip?
  2. Press “Finish strip” four times.
  3. Write down each strip’s total from the inner box.
  4. Why do the outer strips contribute more than the middle ones?
Show answer

4 strips of 4 pieces. The outer strips sit farther from the axis, so their pieces have larger r⊥². Adding the four strip totals gives the full sum.

Exercise 4 — a rod about its end (limiting case)

  1. A 2 kg, 2 m rod has I = ML²/12 about its middle. Predict I about one end.
  2. Choose “Center vs. end”, or drag d to 1.00 m in step 5.
  3. Read ICM, M d² and the total.
  4. Now set the tilt to 90°, so the axis runs along the rod. Why is I zero?
Show answer

0.667 + 2 × 1² = 2.667 kg·m² = ML²/3. Along its own line an ideal rod has every piece on the axis, so r⊥ = 0 for all of them; a real rod has a small nonzero I from its thickness.

Worked example: a solid cylinder about its axis as a triple integral

  1. Locate a piece with three numbers: height z, radius r and angle φ. Its volume is r dr dφ dz, so dm = ρ r dr dφ dz with ρ = M/(πR²H).
  2. Innermost sum, around a ring: every piece has the same r, so the angle sum is ∫ 0 2 π d φ = 2 π .
  3. Middle sum, rings into a slice: ∫ 0 R r 2 ρ 2 π r d r = ρ 2 π R 4 4 .
  4. Outer sum, stacking slices: ∫ d z = H , so I = ρ 2 π H R 4 4 = M π R 2 H · 2 π H R 4 4 = M R 2 2 .
  5. Check in the builder: choose the solid cylinder; the three nested boxes are exactly these three sums.

When the model fails

Thickness: “thin” rods, disks and hoops have zero thickness here; real ones have slightly larger I. Non-uniform bodies: density is uniform; a wheel with a heavy rim needs a density that changes with r. Non-rigid bodies: a skater pulling in their arms changes I while spinning. Free rotation: a body that is not held on a fixed axis can wobble; that needs the full inertia tensor.

Formula sheet: every shape, and where it comes from

I = ∫ r⊥² dm

Every formula below comes from the same recipe. 1. Cut the body into tiny pieces and write the mass of one piece, dm. 2. Write that piece’s distance from the axis, r⊥. 3. Put both into I = ∫ r⊥² dm and add up over the whole body. Use one ∫ for each number that locates a piece.

How do we find λ, σ and ρ?

For a uniform body, equal lengths, areas or volumes contain equal masses. The rule is density = total mass ÷ total length, area or volume occupied by the material. A tiny piece then has dm = density × the piece’s length, area or volume.

Along a line: λ (lambda)

Mass per metre, in kg/m.

λ = M / total length
dm = λ ds

ds is a tiny length. For a rod the total length is L; for a hoop it is the circumference 2πR.

Across a surface: σ (sigma)

Mass per square metre, in kg/m².

σ = M / total area
dm = σ dA

dA is a tiny area. A disk has area πR²; a plate has area LW. For a ring, subtract the hole: π(R² − a²).

Throughout a solid: ρ (rho)

Mass per cubic metre, in kg/m³.

ρ = M / total volume
dm = ρ dV

dV is a tiny volume. A cylinder has volume πR²H; a solid sphere has volume 4πR³/3.

Why divide? All the pieces must add back to the total mass: M = ∫ dm. For a uniform rod, M = ∫ λ dx = λL, so λ = M/L. For a hoop, M = λ(2πR), so λ = M/(2πR). A tiny arc has length ds = R dφ (φ in radians), giving dm = λR dφ = M/(2π) dφ.

Check the units: (kg/m) × m = kg, just as (kg/m²) × m² = kg and (kg/m³) × m³ = kg. Moving or tilting the rotation axis changes r⊥, but keeps the body’s density and dm the same. If density varies with position, use its local value inside the integral; dividing total mass by total size gives only the average.

M is the total mass. R, L, W and H are the body’s radius, length, width and height; a is the ring’s inner radius. φ and θ are angles in radians. “Load” sets the lab to that body and axis with M = 2 kg, R = 1 m, L = 2 m, W = 1 m and H = 2 m, so you can check the number in the stats bar.

Spot the pattern. Every result is M × (a length)² × a number. The number depends only on where the mass sits: 1 when all of it is at the rim (hoop, tube), 1/2 for a filled disk or cylinder, 2/3 for a hollow sphere, 2/5 for a solid one. The more mass near the axis, the smaller the number.
