01

What is moment of inertia?

Moment of inertia, written I, measures how hard it is to change how fast something spins. It is the spinning version of mass.

MOVING IN A STRAIGHT LINE

Mass resists a push

Push a cart with a force F and it speeds up with acceleration a. Load the cart with more mass and the same push speeds it up less.

F=maforce = mass × acceleration
SPINNING ABOUT AN AXIS

Moment of inertia resists a twist

Twist a wheel with a torque τ and its spin speeds up with angular acceleration α. With a larger I, the same twist speeds the spin up less.

τ=Iαtorque = moment of inertia × angular acceleration

There is one big difference. A cart’s mass is just a number: it doesn’t matter where the load sits. For spinning, where the mass sits matters a lot. Mass far from the axis is much harder to spin up than the same mass close to it.

TRY IT · ONE BALL ON A LIGHT ARM

Move the ball. Watch r² grow.

The arm weighs nothing. The ball has mass m and sits a distance r from the axis, which you are looking straight down. The gold square has side r, so its area is r².

I = m × r²0.250 kg·m²

Why distance counts twice (r × r)

  1. Farther away means faster. In one turn, a ball at distance r travels around a circle of length 2πr. To speed the spin up at rate α, the ball itself must speed up at a = rα. Twice as far needs twice the acceleration, so twice the force: F = m·rα.
  2. Turning force works through a lever. A twist (torque) is force × distance from the axis: τ = r·F. Put the two together: τ = r·(m·rα) = (m r²)·α.

Compare τ = (m r²)·α with F = m·a. The quantity m r² plays the role that mass plays for straight-line motion. That is the moment of inertia of one small ball: I = m r².

Single, double or triple integral?

An integral sign ∫ just means “add up”. You need one ∫ for each number it takes to say where a tiny piece is.

1 number · ∫

A thin line

Rod, hoop. Each piece is located by one number: how far along the rod (x), or the angle around the hoop (φ).

dm = λ · dx
λ = mass per metre
2 numbers · ∫∫

A flat surface

Disk, ring, plate. You need two numbers: x and y, or a radius r and an angle φ.

dm = σ · dA
σ = mass per square metre
3 numbers · ∫∫∫

A solid

Cylinder, sphere. Inside a solid you need three numbers, such as x, y and z.

dm = ρ · dV
ρ = mass per cubic metre

“Thin” bodies are idealized: a hoop or disk is treated as having no thickness, which removes one dimension. A real disk has some thickness, but it barely changes how far its mass sits from the axis.

What distance is actually squared?

The axis points along n = (sin β, 0, cos β) and is shifted by d(cos β, 0, −sin β). Distance is measured perpendicular to this line, not to a single point on it. The centered x and z moments combine because these shapes are symmetric about their coordinate planes.

05

What if the axis isn’t through the center?

The integrals above use an axis through the center of mass (CM). Many real objects spin about some other line: a door about its hinges, a pendulum about its pivot, a bat swung from its handle.

Two motions happen at once

  1. The center travels around the axis on a circle of radius d. For this motion the whole body acts like one ball of mass M at distance d. From step 01, that costs M d².
  2. The body also turns about its own center, exactly as it would on the centered axis. That costs ICM, the value from the integral.
I=ICM+Md2parallel-axis theorem
Spin about own center ICM
Center going around M d²
Total I

The curve is I for every axis position, parallel to the current one. It is lowest at d = 0 and rises as a parabola. The motion section shows the CM’s circle (dashed) whenever d ≠ 0.

The center is always the easiest axis.

M d² is never negative. Of all parallel axes, the one through the CM has the smallest I.

Left or right doesn’t matter.

d and −d give the same I, because d is squared.

Example: a rod about its end.

Centered: ML²/12. The end is d = L/2 away, so I = ML²/12 + M(L/2)² = ML²/3: four times harder to spin.

Why do the two parts simply add, with nothing in between?

Measure each piece from the CM: its position is r′, and the axis sits at distance d. Its squared distance to the new axis is |r′ − d|² = r′² − 2 r′·d + d². Add this over every piece: Σ m r′² gives ICM, and Σ m d² gives M d². The middle term is −2d · Σ m r′, and Σ m r′ = 0, because that is exactly what “center of mass” means: the pieces balance around it. So the cross term vanishes, and nothing else is left.

Three things to take with you

Mass sets the scale.

Double the mass with the same geometry and axis, and you double I.

Distance has a squared effect.

Move a mass element twice as far from the axis and its contribution becomes four times larger.

An axis is part of the answer.

A body has different moments about different axes. Tilting and shifting are different operations.
